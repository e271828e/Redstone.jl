6. Composition: connections, aggregation and hierar­
chy
Components (the units of modeling, leaf or assembly) become a system through wiring. Connections 
route signals across the hierarchy of assemblies (components of pure composition, with no dynamics 
of their own). Ordinary junction components sit wherever several signals must combine into one. 
§6.1 gives the connection and hierarchy rules. §6.2 gives the aggregation idiom they force.
6.1 Connections and hierarchy
A wire names its two endpoints by path, and those paths run down the hierarchy of children. How 
far a path may reach decides whether a parent addresses a grandchild's port (one declared input or 
output) directly or every intermediate level must re-export it. That question comes first here. Next 
comes what type-checks a wire once both endpoints resolve. The last part, on connections per port, 
covers how many connections each side may take and what becomes of the ports left unconnected.
How far a path may reach
An assembly (a component of pure composition) has three wiring declarations (§8.6). A local_wires 
pair wires one child's face (the name a port wears on its component's boundary) to another's. An 
input_wires entry routes a face to an immediate child's face. An output_wires entry sources a face 
from an immediate child's face. An entry of any of the three is a wire, and an entry of the first is a 
local wire (D-303, D-314).
Every connection endpoint names an immediate child and one of its faces  ( D-207). An 
endpoint naming no port of the child it resolves to is an UnknownPort error, at either end of the wire 
(D-113, Appendix C). The one-level rule covers all three wiring declarations. Container children 
(the elements of a Tuple or NamedTuple field holding only components, §8.5) keep their key segment 
(D-085), so "aircraft/2/face" is one level and not two ( D-207). A container declared name-trans -
parent (§8.5) is the exception. Its elements go by bare key, so "ctl/face" reads exactly as a plain 
child's endpoint does, the same one level (D-211).
Routing across several levels is therefore declared level by level, with each assembly speaking only 
of its own children. This restriction is named the locality law ( D-065). Fragments of the condition 
algebra follow the same law (§14.2).
The sketch below shows such routing in an aircraft. The root Cessna172 holds a systems assembly 
Systems, which holds the landing gear Ldg, whose three legs are left, right and nose. The root's input 
trn is a terrain field handle (an immutable query object its consumers evaluate, §4.4). Cessna172 
hands its trn input to systems, Systems hands it to ldg, and Ldg fans it out to its three legs. The fan-
out is declared at the level where the paths diverge.
input_wires(::Cessna172) = ("trn" :> "systems/trn", …)
input_wires(::Systems)   = ("trn" :> "ldg/trn", …)
